      Back in July, the Department of Education unleashed these new rules for something they've dubbed the Student Tuition and Transparency System, or STATS for short. Starting in 2027, if a college program's graduates earn less than high school grads for two out of three years, that program gets the boot from federal loans for a couple of years.

      Seems fair, right?

      If colleges are charging an arm and a leg and leaving students floundering in debt with nothing to show for it, someone needs to hold them accountable. The whole notion that "education is priceless" is laughable when it comes to protecting consumers. Just because a college has a fancy Latin motto doesn't mean it gets a free pass.

      But here's the kicker: thinking a single salary number can sum up whether a degree is "worth it" is a bit of a stretch.

      When folks ask if a degree was worth it, they're really digging into a few things: Was the education worth a dime? Was it reasonably priced? Does it lead to a decent job?

      These are all different questions.

      Gallup and Lumina put out some data in 2026 that shows this mismatch. Most graduates—71%—felt their education was worth it, and a whopping 93% would do it all over again. But only 11% thought the price was fair, and more than half said their debt delayed life choices. People can hold two thoughts at once: their education mattered, but the price was ridiculous.

      Federal policy doesn't have that luxury. It needs a straightforward number that works across the board. Salaries are tempting because they're easy to measure and can't be spun with marketing fluff.

      But just because you can measure it doesn't make it the whole picture.

      A salary tells you something, just not everything.

      Researchers like Sandy Baum and Kristin Blagg warn about judging programs based solely on earnings. You've got to differentiate between a bad program and one that just leads to a lower-paying field.

      This is a big deal in areas like education, healthcare, and the arts—important jobs that don't always pay well.

      Think about a teacher program that churns out amazing educators. If they're underpaid, who's to blame?

      Was the program bad?
      Was the tuition too high?
      Does the market undervalue teaching?
      Or maybe it's all of the above?

      A paycheck doesn't clear this up. That's the nuance that's often overlooked.

      The real work begins after all the human decisions.

      Labeling a policy "data-driven" can make political talk sound neutral, like the numbers are unbiased.

      But numbers come from human choices.

      The federal earnings framework is packed with judgments: Who counts as a graduate? When do we measure their earnings? Who's in the comparison group? How do we handle small programs without messing up the data?

      Analysts at the Urban Institute point out there are trade-offs. Mixing years and programs to get a decent sample size blends different majors or leans on outdated data.

      That doesn't make the metric useless, just man-made.

      "Data-driven" doesn't mean "value-free." The math starts after deciding what matters.

      Now throw in the labor market's inequalities.

      Things get messier when grads step into an unfair economy.

      The Urban Institute noted a blind spot: students with criminal records. IRS data often misses wages for these folks, which could unfairly penalize programs serving them.

      Does the rule punish programs giving second chances? Not necessarily. But it raises a tough question: if grads earn less because of employer bias against criminal records, who's to blame?

      The grad?
      The college?
      The employer?
      The justice system?

      A record follows people after their sentence. Feeding wage suppression into program assessments turns discrimination into perceived failure.

      This isn't saying we shouldn't track outcomes.

      It's about acknowledging that numbers don't explain themselves.

      Colleges need to be held accountable.

      There's a lazy argument: Education's about more than money, so earnings shouldn't matter. That's rubbish.

      If students take on debt, earnings matter. If a program traps people in debt, that matters. If elite schools know their grads struggle financially and still hike tuition, that matters a lot.

      The Department of Education's got the right idea. Colleges shouldn't hide poor economic outcomes behind vague claims of personal growth or the "college experience."

      The solution isn't to ditch accountability but to make it better.

      Earnings should be part of the picture, along with tuition costs, debt, graduation rates, exams, and info students can understand before they sign on the dotted line.

      The challenge is not letting one number carry all the weight.
      A low salary could point to a lousy program.
      It could mean predatory tuition.
      It could be about a low-paying career or discrimination.

      The policy problem isn't tracking earnings. It's pretending they tell the whole story.

      Who gets to decide what a degree's worth?

      This debate is more than just numbers on a spreadsheet.

      Once earnings benchmarks start affecting federal aid for certain paths, the definition of "value" has real consequences. It decides which programs survive, which get axed, which fields become financial risks for universities.

      This needs more thought than just a simple yes or no.

      A paycheck tells you something about life after graduation. Alone, it doesn't reveal if the college failed, the price was too high, the market broke, or society undervalues the work.

      When a number becomes the gatekeeper, we've got to be honest about the story it tells.
