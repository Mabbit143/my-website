Mabbit investigates

Somebody wrote the rules

Mabbit's second look

"Critically analyze." Great. Analyze what, using whose evidence, judged by which rubric?

A prompt is a set of rules written by someone who already knows what a good answer looks like. Your job is to get that out of their head and onto your page.

  1. Which words tell me what to do, and which only name the topic?
  2. What will I be graded on that nobody wrote down?
  3. Where is "good" defined? A rubric, an example, the syllabus? Or only in the instructor's head?

What task words usually ask for

Starting guesses, not a standard. Instructors use these loosely, so confirm yours.

Common assignment task words and what they usually ask the writer to do
Task wordUsually asks you to
Describe, summarizeReport accurately what is there. No verdict needed.
ExplainSay how or why something works, with reasons, not just facts.
AnalyzeBreak something into parts and show what each part does or how they connect.
CompareSet two or more things against stated criteria, then say what the comparison shows.
Argue, evaluateTake a position, defend it with evidence, and deal with the strongest objection.
ReflectConnect the material to your own experience or thinking and say what changed.

Whonah's field notes

Mark it up. In this order.

Whonah works the trail one scent at a time. So do you. Four passes, four marks, pen on paper.

  1. Underline every verb. Analyze, compare, address. These are your tasks. Violet underline.
  2. Circle every number and format rule. 1,200 words four sources APA. Length, sources, style, file type, deadline. Pink circle.
  3. Box must, should, and may. must is required. should and may are where you need an answer. Cyan box.
  4. Find the grading criteria. Rubric, syllabus section, or an example. Write down where you found it. If you can't find it, that is your first question.

Checkpoint

  • I know the action I'm being asked to do.
  • I know the evidence I have to use, and how much.
  • I know the format rules and the deadline.
  • I know where the grading criteria live, or I know I have to ask.

Whonah's rule: if a word could mean two things, mark it. A marked word is a question you can send.

The evidence board · case 01

The assignment autopsy

Sample prompt written for this guide. It is not from a real course. Your instructor's wording will differ.

Analyze how one policy affected a community you choose. Write a 1,200-word paper using at least four scholarly sources. You should address multiple perspectives. Cite in APA.
underline = taskcircle = number or format rulebox = vague or required word

Mabbit reads it

  • Task: "analyze" means take the policy apart and show its effect on the community. Naming the policy is not enough.
  • Evidence: four sources, and "scholarly" is undefined. Do reports from agencies count? Somebody has that answer.
  • Hidden assumption: "multiple perspectives" assumes you know whose voices are missing. That is on you to find.

Whonah's marks

  • Hard rules: 1,200 words, 4+ sources, APA.
  • Fog: "should" (required or advised?), "multiple" (is two enough?), "scholarly" (what counts?).
  • Missing: deadline, rubric, who the audience is.

First move

"Do government reports count as scholarly sources, and is two perspectives enough? Is there a rubric I can see before I start?"

One message, two specific questions, sent before the work starts.

Check your work

Did it work?

Whonah is looking at your page. Check it against these. If an answer is no, go back to that pass.

Criteria

  • Every line of the prompt has a mark, or I can say why it's only topic.
  • My task list uses the prompt's own verbs, not my paraphrase.
  • I found at least one vague word, or I asked what "done" looks like.
  • Each question I wrote can be answered in one or two sentences.
  • Someone else could start the assignment from my list.

Common pitfalls

Marking only the topic."Policy" and "community" are topic words. The verbs tell you what to do with them. Go back to Pass 1.
Reading "should" as optional.Instructors often use "should" to mean required, and sometimes they don't. That's why it gets a question, not a guess.
Asking a huge question."What do you want?" is hard to answer. "Does a government report count as scholarly?" is not.
Waiting to ask.A question sent before the work starts costs less than a rewrite after you've turned it in.

Model answer for the sample prompt

Tasks: analyze effects; address multiple perspectives. Rules: 1,200 words, four or more scholarly sources, APA. Fog: should, multiple, scholarly. Question: "Do agency reports count as scholarly, and is two perspectives enough?" Yours won't match word for word. It should match in kind.

The final verdict

What you found out

Finding

The prompt is a rulebook. Read it like one.

Verbs are tasks. Numbers are rules. "Should," "multiple," and "scholarly" are questions waiting for the person who wrote them.

What this can't tell you

  • How your instructor will grade. Only they, or the rubric, can say.
  • Whether a task word means what the table says in your course.
  • What a strong answer looks like. Ask for an example.

Do next

  • Send your message.
  • Save the answer where you can find it.
  • Check the rest of the course rules with Read the Fine Print.

Reflect

Which word in my prompt was doing the most work without being defined?
